Forces: GCSE Physics revision

23 short interactive lessons. Every answer gets instant feedback and a "Why?".

Open Upthrust

Not every lesson is on every course. When you start, Upthrust shows only the lessons on yours.

  • Forces as arrows

    By the end, you can say:

    A force is an arrow: its length is the size and the way it points is the direction. I can name it, and say whether the two objects are touching.

  • Weight and gravity

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out the weight of something anywhere, as long as I know its mass and how hard gravity pulls there.

  • Centre of mass

    By the end, you can say:

    I can mark the one point a weight arrow should start from, and say what that point means.

  • Resultant forces

    By the end, you can say:

    I can add every force on an object into one resultant, and say which way it points.

  • Distance-time graphs

    By the end, you can say:

    I can read a journey off a distance-time graph, and find how fast something was going at any point on it.

  • Acceleration

    By the end, you can say:

    If I know three of starting speed, final speed, time, distance and acceleration, I can work out the one that is missing.

  • Velocity-time graphs

    By the end, you can say:

    I can get two things off a velocity-time graph: how fast it is speeding up, from the steepness, and how far it has gone, from the area underneath.

  • Newton's first law

    By the end, you can say:

    If the forces on something are balanced, its velocity does not change. It stays still if it was still, and keeps going if it was going.

  • Newton's second law

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out any one of resultant force, mass or acceleration when I am given the other two.

  • The trolley practical

    By the end, you can say:

    I can plot a set of readings, fit a line to them, say what it shows, and say what I would change to make the readings better.

  • Newton's third law

    By the end, you can say:

    I can point at both objects in a pair and say which force acts on which.

  • Falling and reaching a steady speed

    By the end, you can say:

    I can explain, step by step, how a falling object ends up at a steady falling speed.

  • Momentum

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out momentum, and explain what happens to it when things collide.

  • Force and change in momentum

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out a force from a change in momentum, and explain how a safety feature makes that force smaller.

  • Work done

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out the work done by multiplying the force by how far the thing moved in the direction of that force, in metres.

  • Stopping distances

    By the end, you can say:

    I can say which distance a factor changes, and which way it changes it.

  • Braking and energy

    By the end, you can say:

    I can say where a braking car's energy goes, naming the store it leaves and the store it ends up in.

  • Springing back, or not

    By the end, you can say:

    I can say whether something will spring back when the force is taken off, and why.

  • Springs and the spring constant

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out how stiff a spring is from the graph of force against how far it stretches.

  • Moments

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out a moment, say which way a beam turns, and say why a longer spanner shifts a stiff nut.

  • Levers and gears

    By the end, you can say:

    I can say why moving a force further from the pivot makes it easier to turn something.

  • Pressure on a surface

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out the pressure under something, using the area that is actually touching.

  • Pressure in a liquid

    By the end, you can say:

    I can work out the pressure at a depth in a liquid, and say why something floats.
